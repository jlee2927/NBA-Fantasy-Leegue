01 / THE PROJECTION

Three seasons, not one.

Last season alone is a noisy guess: a player who shot unusually well for one year is unlikely to repeat it. So three seasons are blended, the most recent counting most, and the result is pulled part of the way back toward league average.

How far back depends on the category. Assists are stable, so a player's own assist rate is mostly believed. Three-point percentage swings wildly year to year, so it gets dragged much closer to average. That amount is fitted from past seasons, not chosen by hand.

SHOW THE CALCULATION 
WEIGHTS = (5, 4, 3)              # Y-1, Y-2, Y-3

wstat = sum(w * wide[s].get(y, 0) for w, y in zip(WEIGHTS, yrs))
rate  = (wstat + k[s] * lg_rate[s]) / (wmin + k[s])

wstat is the player's counting stat over three seasons, weighted 5–4–3. wmin is their minutes weighted the same way. Dividing one by the other would give a plain per-minute rate.

The k terms are what make it a projection. Adding k minutes of league-average production to the top and k minutes to the bottom is the same as saying "pretend this player also played k minutes of exactly average basketball." A small k trusts the player; a large one buries them in the league average. Each category gets its own, fitted on earlier seasons only.

02 / AGEING

Players are not static.

A 23-year-old and a 34-year-old coming off identical seasons are not identical bets. Rather than assume a curve, the curve is measured: every player who appeared in two consecutive seasons is compared against himself, and the average change at each age becomes the adjustment.

Turnovers are left alone. The method assumes more is better, which is false for the one category where fewer is better.

SHOW THE CALCULATION 
# Pairs weighted by the harmonic mean of both seasons' minutes,
# so a pair of full seasons counts more than a pair of cameos.
a["w"] = 2 / (1 / a.min1 + 1 / a.min2)

y = np.log(b.n / b.d)
coef = np.polyfit(b.index, y, 2, w=np.sqrt(b.w))
out[s] = np.exp(np.polyval(coef, ages))

Comparing a player only against himself removes the problem that good players last longer: if you simply averaged every 34-year-old, you would be averaging the survivors and ageing would look painless.

Four or five one-year transitions leave some ages very thin, so the raw factors are smoothed with a weighted quadratic fitted in log space — log because the effect is multiplicative, and fitting it as a straight sum would let one noisy age bucket swing the whole curve.

03 / PLAYING TIME

Minutes and games, separately.

A rate is worth nothing without the playing time to apply it to, and availability is its own skill. Games are projected as a share of the schedule rather than a count, which matters because not every season has been 82 games: the 2012 lockout ran 66, and the 2020 and 2021 seasons were cut short too.

SHOW THE CALCULATION 
s1, s2 = wide["g_share"].get(yrs[0], 0), wide["g_share"].get(yrs[1], 0)
out["proj_g"] = np.clip(0.5 * s1 + 0.1 * s2 + game_base, 0, 1) * FULL_SEASON

Half of last season's availability, a tenth of the season before, plus a baseline. The baseline is what stops a player who missed almost everything from being projected at almost nothing — most of them come back. For the 2026 season the fitted values were a game_base of 0.225 and a minutes baseline of 500.

Storing games as a share is what lets a 66-game lockout season and an 82-game season inform the same baseline without one dragging the other down.

04 / VALUATION

Every category counts the same.

A projection is a pile of statistics; a draft needs one number. Each category is converted into standard deviations from the average of the draftable pool, so scarcity decides worth rather than raw size.

That is why one extra steal per game beats eighteen extra points. Almost every draftable player gets about one steal, so two is genuinely rare — while one more point separates nobody.

SHOW THE CALCULATION 
sd = ref[c].std(ddof=0)
z[c] = (values[c] - ref[c].mean()) / sd
if c in NEGATIVE:                # turnovers: fewer is better
    z[c] = -z[c]

z["total"] = z[list(cats)].mean(axis=1)

Percentages need care. A 100% shooter on one attempt has not helped anybody, so the ratio categories are scored by how far a player moves the team total, which scales with volume: 

out[c] = df[made] - (ref[made].sum() / ref[att].sum()) * df[att]

Written that way rather than as (pct - league_pct) * attempted because the two are algebraically identical, and this form leaves a player with zero attempts at zero impact instead of dividing by zero.

There is a circularity to resolve: the averages should come from draftable players, but the z-scores are what decide who is draftable. The pool is seeded by minutes and re-ranked until it stops moving. 

pool = df.nlargest(n, "proj_mpg").index
for _ in range(MAX_PASSES):
    z = z_scores(category_values(df, cats, pool), cats, pool)
    nxt = z[list(cats)].sum(axis=1).nlargest(n).index
    if frozenset(nxt) == frozenset(pool):
        break
    pool = nxt
05 / PUNTING

Giving one up on purpose.

In a category league you win a week by taking most of the categories, not by being adequate at all of them. Deciding in advance to lose one frees you to stop paying for it — which is why a high-turnover guard climbs the board the moment turnovers stop counting.

Mechanically a punt is just dropping that category out of the average. Everything else is unchanged; the board simply re-sorts.

SHOW THE CALCULATION

The mean is taken over the categories still being contested, so a punted category contributes nothing rather than counting as a loss. Using the mean rather than the sum is also what keeps an 8-category score comparable with a 9-category one, and stops punting three categories from deflating every number on the board.

Builds are capped at four punts in 9-cat. Punting five means giving up the majority before the draft starts, which is losing every week by construction.
